Morphological image processing

Study notes covering slides 1–33: set-theoretic foundations, erosion and dilation, opening and closing, and the hit-or-miss transform. Toggle pixels in the lab below to see the operations run on your own shape.

01 — Foundations

What morphology is doing

Morphological processing treats an image as a set of pixels — foreground versus background — and probes it with a small set called a structuring element (SE). The SE slides across the image; at each position a logical test decides the output pixel. Different SEs surface different structures.

Three operations on sets

OperationMeaningFormula
ReflectionRotate SE 180° about its originB̂ = {w | w = −b, b ∈ B}
TranslationShift a set by z(B)ᵤ = {c | c = b+z, b ∈ B}
ComplementEverything not in A (i.e. the background)Aᶜ = {z | z ∉ A}

The general recipe: start with an all-background output image, slide the SE over the input, and at each position write a foreground or background pixel at the SE's origin based on a logical test between the SE and the pixels underneath it. This is structurally the same shape as convolution — except the operation is a set comparison, not a weighted sum.

02 — The two primitives

Erosion and dilation

Every other morphological operation is built from these two.

Erosion

A ⊖ B = {z | (B)ᵤ ⊆ A}

Keeps a position only if the entire SE fits inside the foreground.

  • Shrinks foreground regions
  • Removes thin protrusions and small specks
  • No reflection of B needed

Dilation

A ⊕ B = {z | (B̂)ᵤ ∩ A ≠ ∅}

Keeps a position if the reflected SE overlaps the foreground by even one pixel.

  • Grows foreground regions
  • Fills small gaps and breaks
  • Uses the reflected SE, B̂

Duality

Erosion and dilation are complements of each other:

(A ⊖ B)ᶜ = Aᶜ ⊕ B̂ (A ⊕ B)ᶜ = Aᶜ ⊖ B̂

Eroding the foreground is the same as dilating the background, and vice versa. This is most useful when the SE is symmetric (B = B̂), since the reflection can be dropped.

04 — Compound operations

Opening and closing

Opening

A ∘ B = (A ⊖ B) ⊕ B
  • Smooths contours
  • Breaks narrow isthmuses
  • Eliminates thin protrusions

Geometric view: the union of every position where B fits fully inside A — roll the SE around inside the shape.

Closing

A ● B = (A ⊕ B) ⊖ B
  • Smooths contours
  • Fuses narrow breaks
  • Fills small holes and gaps

Geometric view: the complement of every SE position that misses A entirely — seals off gaps from the outside.

Worked case: fingerprint cleanup

A noisy fingerprint has both salt (stray foreground specks) and pepper (stray background gaps) noise. Erosion alone removes salt but thins the ridges; dilation alone restores ridges but leaves pepper noise. Closing (dilate, then erode) cleans up most of the pepper noise while limiting ridge distortion — this is why the compound operations, not the raw primitives, get used for denoising.

05 — Shape detection

Hit-or-miss transform

Erosion alone only checks the foreground, which isn't enough to pin down a specific shape — a larger object containing that shape would match too. The hit-or-miss transform (HMT) also checks the surrounding background.

Two-SE form

Define B₁ to match the foreground and B₂ to match the background around it:

I ⊛ B₁,₂ = (A ⊖ B₁) ∩ (Aᶜ ⊖ B₂)

Erode A by B₁, erode Aᶜ by B₂, and intersect the results. Only positions where both conditions hold survive — the object's exact location.

Single-SE form (slide 33)

Instead of two separate SEs and erosions, let one SE carry foreground, background, and don't-care values, and apply it directly to the image rather than just the foreground set:

I ⊛ B = {z | (B)ᵤ ⊆ I}

A single erosion-like pass now checks both constraints at once — the SE itself encodes what must be foreground and what must be background.

07 — Test yourself

Self-check

Why does dilation use the reflected SE, B̂, while erosion doesn't?

Dilation is defined as the dual of erosion under complement — reflecting the SE keeps the duality identities consistent. For symmetric SEs (B = B̂), the distinction disappears in practice.

What happens to a thin protrusion under opening versus closing?

Opening removes it (the SE can't fit inside the thin part, so erosion wipes it out and dilation can't bring back what's gone). Closing leaves it untouched or even connects it more firmly, since closing targets gaps, not protrusions.

Why does closing help with "pepper" noise but not "salt" noise?

Closing dilates first, which fills in small background dropouts (pepper) surrounded by foreground; the following erosion then restores the original boundary size. Salt noise (stray foreground specks) needs erosion first — that's what opening does.

Why is checking only the foreground (plain erosion) insufficient for locating a specific shape?

A larger object that happens to contain the target shape's foreground pattern would also survive the erosion test. Without also checking that the surrounding background matches, you can't distinguish the shape from something bigger that merely contains it.

How does a single 3-valued SE replace the two-SE hit-or-miss transform?

The SE is allowed to specify foreground, background, and don't-care positions all at once, and it's applied directly to the whole image (not just the foreground set). One containment check per position then verifies both the foreground match and the background match simultaneously, replacing the two separate erosions and their intersection.
